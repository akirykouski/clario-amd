## Business value

**Buyer.** The clinic or hospital department. **User on the other side.** The patient, free.

**What the clinic gets.** A structured pre-visit dossier (terms the patient did not reject, with the patient's own quotes, a timeline, imported lab history) and a brief the clinician edits and signs, where every AI line carries a citation.

**Value hypothesis, to be measured.** The brief reduces the time a clinician spends rebuilding the history at the start of a visit. We do not claim a number. The pilot measures minutes to first useful question with and without the dossier, and how many AI lines the clinician keeps, edits or removes when signing (the clinic console counts them per brief, with the time from opening a package to signing, at `GET /api/clinic/metrics`).

**Pricing hypothesis, untested.** Per clinic subscription for the clinic console and the on-premise model server, set after pilot interviews. The patient app stays free so that dossiers keep arriving. A hospital that prefers not to run its own server can use a managed clinic instance under a data processing agreement.

**Go-to-market in Italy.**

1. Start with reference centres for rare and undiagnosed diseases, where the diagnostic wait is longest and the visit is already a structured, multi-specialty process.
2. Work through patient associations (for example UNIAMO, the Italian federation of rare diseases) to recruit pilot patients, using the synthetic demo first.
3. Run a 90-day pilot at one or two centres with a measurement plan agreed upfront.
4. Italian and English from day one (the app and the data generator already handle both).
5. Expand by reference network once acceptance and time data exist.

## Judge Q&A prep (15 questions)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is this a medical device? | Clario organises information. It does not diagnose and gives no treatment advice, and a clinician edits and signs every brief. Our position is that this scope is outside the EU Medical Device Regulation, pending a regulatory assessment with a qualified advisor before any clinical use. Code enforces the scope: hedge validator, no condition name ever shown to the patient, no percentages. The clinician view names conditions worth discussing, which is the part a regulator would study most. Detail in `docs/COMPLIANCE.md`. Not legal advice |
| 2 | Why not just use GPT or Claude? | Three reasons. Health text is Article 9 data, so we use open-weights models that a clinic can run on its own server (the roadmap), and both sides replace direct identifiers by rules before any remote call (misses 10.8% of identifiers on our strictly held-out synthetic test half, 5.8% after fixes chosen by reading test failures, `eval/results/pii_leak.json`, n = 712). Second, a small fine-tuned model (E4B) beat the larger models we ran on our task (0.60 entity F1 against 0.40 for DeepSeek-V4-Flash on the same 100 synthetic rows, `eval/RESULTS.md`), and it is small enough for a patient's laptop; the Ryzen AI build is not yet tested on hardware. Third, the guardrails are in code (quotes, ontology, citations), so the base model matters less than the checks around it. We use Claude only to draft gold labels for human review and as an eval comparison, never for training |
| 3 | Where does the data go? | The diary stays in an encrypted vault on the patient's device. A share sends only the entries from a chosen period and their terms (never the ones the patient rejected), sealed to the clinic's key, expiring. The clinic stores the opened package on its own server. The privacy page lists every model call and its hash chain. Before text reaches a remote model, Clario replaces direct identifiers with placeholders using rules and a first-name lexicon, with no model involved: emails, phone numbers, codice fiscale, tessera sanitaria and hospital record numbers, street addresses, full dates including dates of birth, and personal names recognised by title, label, relation, first-name list or "X said" pattern. Medical eponyms, doses, lab values, drug names and places of care are kept. On a synthetic English and Italian test set it misses 10.8% of identifiers when strictly held out, and 5.8% after fixes chosen by reading test failures (`eval/results/pii_leak.json`, n = 712); first names that open a sentence and are not in the lexicon are the largest gap. Scanned images and photos are sent unredacted; the preview shows the exact text the model receives. The clinic's brief prompt is pseudonymised the same way, with dates turned into day numbers. On the public demo everything is synthetic |
| 4 | How do you know the model is right? | We check it. Each term must quote the patient's words, each ontology id must match those words, and uncertain ones are flagged for the clinician. On the numbers: 0.629 entity F1 and 0.998 grounding on 300 synthetic test diaries held out by disease (`eval/results/extraction_ft-e4b.json`). Known limits: the test set is synthetic and from the same generator as the training data, and family history is weak (relative recall 0.139). No real-text result exists yet |
| 5 | Your training data is synthetic. Does it transfer? | Unproven, and we say so. Labels come from the ontology by construction, with disease-disjoint splits. A hand-reviewed gold set and PsyTAR mapped to HPO are set up (`train/gold_draft.py`, `train/psytar_hpo.py`) but have no result file yet, so we quote no real-text number |
| 6 | What stops it from diagnosing? | Four layers. Condition names are clinician-only: patients see one card titled by the features they recorded, in everyday words. Pattern scores and the patient text come from code and a template, and the model never sees a condition name for the patient. A validator rejects "you have", "confirms", doses and percentages and falls back to a template. A clinician edits and signs the brief before it is used; the draft PDF says "AI draft, not reviewed" until then |
| 7 | What did AMD actually do here? | On AMD Token Factory: Gemma 4 31B wrote our training data (published on Hugging Face, m0rtyddd/clario-synth-v3), MinerU2.5-Pro reads the lab reports (48 of 48 rows on the 3 demo reports, `eval/results/documents_tf-mineru-table.json`), Gemma 4 31B drafts the clinician brief from pseudonymised input, and the large-model benchmark ran there. The fine-tune and the E4B serving ran on Modal NVIDIA (L40S, L4), because AMD Developer Cloud credits never arrived. The ROCm training script and an MI300X runbook are ready. Next on AMD: the patient model on Ryzen AI via Lemonade, and the brief on the clinic's own Instinct servers |
| 8 | Why would a clinic pay? | Our hypothesis is time and quality at the start of the visit. We have not measured it. The pilot is designed to measure minutes to first useful question and how many AI lines clinicians keep when signing |
| 9 | What about a wrong or missing term or lab value? | Missing terms are the patient's to add, wrong ones the patient or clinician rejects. A lab reading that fails a code check waits for the clinician, who confirms, rejects or corrects it, and signing waits for it. In the brief, a line that names a term its cited notes do not record is shown as a warning in "Edit and sign". Every decision writes a correction record |
| 10 | What happens in an emergency? | The diary is not an emergency service and says so. Text that describes chest pain with warning signs, trouble breathing, stroke signs, heavy bleeding, a long seizure or self-harm gets an immediate notice with 112 before anything else. A dosing interceptor exists in `backend/clario/safety.py` (`check_entry`): a question about doses, stopping or changing a medicine, or a medicine in pregnancy gets an "ask your clinician or pharmacist" notice, is saved, and never reaches a model. AI text with dose or treatment wording is rejected by the validator |
| 11 | Is this just Phenomizer or LIRICAL? | No, they solve a different problem, and we say where the overlap is. Phenomizer ranks diseases by information-content semantic similarity between a clinician's HPO query and disease annotations, with a p-value from random queries. LIRICAL ranks diseases by likelihood ratios per observed and excluded phenotype, optionally with genotype, and reports a post-test probability. Both take a clinician's term list as input. Clario is a patient-side capture and a clinician-gated brief: the patient writes lay text, sees each term next to the quoted words and rejects what is wrong, and the clinician edits and signs the brief. Our ranker (`backend/clario/patterns.py`) scores information-content weighted best-match overlap against HPO disease annotations, counting close matches only, with at least three specific terms for a card, and shows weak, moderate or strong. It reports no probability. It is decision support for the clinician only, and the patient sees no condition name |
| 12 | Why not use a likelihood-ratio method for the ranking? | A likelihood ratio needs trustworthy frequency data and a prior. Most HPO annotations carry no frequency (the build writes a default of 0.5 when there is none) and our input is patient-reported and noisy, so a post-test probability would claim more than we know. We do not report one. We have not benchmarked our ranker against Phenomizer or LIRICAL on a shared case set, and no such comparison exists in `eval/results`. Doing it on public phenopacket cases is future work |
| 13 | Doesn't the first doctor's opinion bias the second? | That is why the second opinion hides it. The package is split in code into facts and conclusions; the new doctor sees the diary, labs, what was tried and with what effect, signs their own brief, and only then can compare. Uploaded letters can still carry a previous diagnosis: stripping those sections is planned, not built |
| 15 | How do you know the person opening the file is a doctor? | Two checks, and we say what each proves. The doctor types name, surname and province, the fields the public Albo Unico search uses (the Ordine number repeats across provinces, so it is not a national key), and gets a register line such as "Rossi Maria, Ordine di Milano, Albo medici chirurghi". That proves the name is enrolled, not that the person holding the phone is that doctor. The second check is the patient: she sees that line on her own screen and taps Confirm with the doctor in front of her. Only then do the conclusions open, in that doctor's tab, for this visit; closing the tab ends it and the open is logged under that register line. In the demo the register is a list of synthetic doctors; a pilot has a person check the public site, because the machine API sits on PDND for public administrations. No clinic contract is needed (`docs/doctor-verify.md`) |
| 14 | Why not route doubtful terms to the big model? | Our benchmark shows the fine-tuned 4B ahead of every larger model we ran on this task, so a bigger model would not be a better judge. The small model extracts, the clinician reviews |
